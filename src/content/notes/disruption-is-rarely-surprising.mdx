It is rare that an organization is disrupted by a force that it doesn’t see coming at all.

What happens more often, instead, is that the organization sees the disruption coming, but it does not or cannot respond adequately nevertheless.

This can happen for a number of reasons:

- **Responding to the disruption requires an investment that the organization is unwilling to make.** Example: in 2004-5, Blockbuster attempted to fight Netflix by removing late fees and launching Blockbuster Online/Total Access. Because the two programs were too expensive, their CEO (John Antioco) was ousted, and his successor (Jim Keyes) brought back the late fees and let Total Access die.
- **The organization does not have the necessary technical or organizational skills to respond.** Example: Nokia understood the iPhone was a threat, but they were still unable to respond due to technical incompetence (MeeGo, Symbian’s successor, was delayed by 6-12 months) and a culture of fear that prevented information from flowing freely within the organization.
- **The disruption destroys the organization’s entire ecosystem.** Example: Kodak’s profits came from film, chemicals, and prints. With the advent of digital cameras first and smartphones later, there was no space left for Kodak to compete in. For Kodak to remain relevant, it would have had to transform into an entirely different organization. [[pick-your-playground|Its playground was gone.]]
