<Tldr>

- AI won’t take over whole professions. It takes over _execution_, which means turning a known intent into output. It leaves _strategy_, which means deciding what the intent should be.
- It does so in a predictable order. Execution goes before strategy, and necessities go before advantages. Necessities still involve judgment, but nobody competes on them, so that judgment converges on the standard answer. The standard answer is what an LLM produces.
- Whether a profession is at risk depends on the company, not the profession. The same engineer is an advantage at a software company and a necessity at a consumer brand.
- To stay relevant, move up into work that is a source of competitive advantage, or will become one. Then move right, from execution toward strategy.
- Strategy may not be safe forever, but the work that depends most on unique context goes last.

</Tldr>

## 1. When factories stopped making their own power

Around 1900, a factory that wanted electricity had to generate it. Power was a capability you built in-house: your own generators, your own plant, your own staff to run it. In 1909, 64% of the electric motor capacity in US manufacturing still ran on power generated on site.[^1]

Then central stations took over. Samuel Insull’s Commonwealth Edison in Chicago showed that large utilities with big steam turbines could make power for less than any factory could make it for itself.[^2][^3] By 1919, 57% of the electricity used in US manufacturing came from utilities.[^1] For almost every factory, power was a necessity and not something they competed on, so generating it went from a capability to a line item. The capability didn’t disappear. It [[capabilities-move-to-those-for-whom-theyre-the-product|moved to the companies for whom power _was_ the product]].

<Figure name="factory-power" />

AI is doing the same to knowledge work, which is why “Will AI replace designers, engineers, writers, lawyers?” is the wrong question. The same profession can be safe in one company and at risk in the next, and two people in the same profession, at the same company, can face very different futures. What matters is what kind of work you do, and what that work is for. This essay builds on those two ideas.

## 2. Strategy and execution are a spectrum

Start with the distinction:

- **Execution** is turning a known intent into output. AI is strongest here, because someone has already specified the intent.
- **Strategy** is deciding what the intent should be: what’s worth doing, in what order, and why. Original insight comes from this kind of work.

Vaughn Tan calls the second half _meaningmaking_, which he defines as “any decision we make about the subjective value of a thing.” His claim is that “every single one of the important and difficult decisions we make in business — including ‘judgment calls’ and ‘moral decisions’ — requires meaningmaking,” and that “only humans can do meaningmaking work, while machines cannot do meaningmaking work at all (for now).”[^4]

Throughout this essay, “strategy” means meaningmaking in Tan’s sense. It’s a function, not a job title, and it happens well outside the boardroom and the consultant’s slide deck. A designer defining a brand’s visual language and an engineer choosing what the system should do are both doing strategy in this sense, because the core of their work is deciding what’s worth making rather than making it.

This matches my own experience. AI is good at _translating_ between domains, such as English into code, text into images, or long text into short text. It’s also good at generating output once the intent is clear. It is not good at _creating meaning_: deciding which items matter most, understanding what is worth doing at all, or finding the most effective route from where you are to where you want to be. [[execution-is-translation-strategy-is-meaningmaking|Execution is translation, and strategy is meaningmaking]].

This isn’t a binary. Every piece of work sits somewhere [[strategy-and-execution-are-a-spectrum|on a spectrum]], depending on how much of the intent is still open when it reaches you. In software, the spectrum runs through typing out a function someone else specified, implementing a feature from a spec, designing the system behind it, choosing which features to build, and choosing which customers to build for. Each step down that list involves less translation and more meaningmaking. Almost no work is pure translation, since even typing out a specified function involves small judgment calls. But the more of the intent is already settled, the less meaningmaking is left to do.

Very few jobs sit at a single point. Most people do a mix, and the mix is what matters, because AI takes over the spectrum starting from the execution end.

## 3. Necessities converge on the standard answer

Now look at what the work is for:

- **Necessities** are functions a company needs but doesn’t compete on. They have a [[necessities-have-a-point-of-diminishing-returns|point of diminishing returns]]. Past “good enough”, the next dollar is better spent elsewhere.
- **Advantages** are functions a company competes on. An advantage has to be [[efficiency-must-be-defensible|defensible]], so it must come from some [[strategy-requires-original-insights|original insight]].[^5] It can’t come from the model, for the same reason [[economies-of-scale-are-not-exclusive|economies of scale are not exclusive]]. Anything available to everyone is an advantage to no one. In terms of the [[competitive-advantage-is-your-value-cost-gap|value-cost gap]], AI lowers cost for everyone, so it can’t widen anyone’s gap on its own.

Few functions are pure necessities or pure advantages. Most contribute _something_ to how a company competes, just more or less of it. The split also has exceptions. A function that’s usually a necessity can briefly become decisive during a replatforming, a crisis, or a regulatory change, when [[regulation-can-create-opportunity|regulation can create opportunity]] for whoever handles it best. And a function that’s an advantage overall has plenty of corners that are pure upkeep.

This is where the two spectrums meet. Where a function sits on this one largely decides where its work sits on the other. Necessity work still involves judgment about what counts as good enough, which trade-offs to accept, and what this particular contract needs. But it’s [[necessities-converge-on-the-standard-answer|judgment with a standard answer]]. Since the company doesn’t compete on the function, there’s no reason to differ from what everyone else does, so the meaningmaking converges on the same store, the same process, and the same contract everyone else has. An intent settled by convention is still a known intent, and turning a known intent into output is execution, even when it feels like judgment to the person doing it. And an LLM produces the standard answer, because it has absorbed every example of it.

So the two spectrums move together without being the same thing. The less a function contributes to competitive advantage, the sooner it hits the point of diminishing returns, the more of its meaningmaking the market has already done, and the further its work slides toward the execution end.

At the advantage end, the opposite holds. The intent stays open, because differing from the standard answer is the whole point. [[all-good-strategies-are-unique|All good strategies are unique]], and [[best-practices-are-a-floor-not-a-ceiling|best practices are a floor, not a ceiling]]. The company competes on the original insight. And since every competitor has access to the same models, AI raises the baseline for everyone without giving anyone an edge. The edge still has to come from the insight.

Won’t cheaper execution create more demand for necessities? It will. Companies will review more contracts, produce more content, and answer more support tickets. But the extra demand is for the standard answer, so AI meets it, not the people who used to provide it.

## 4. The same profession, a different company

[[whether-a-function-is-an-advantage-depends-on-the-company|Where a profession lands depends on the company]], not the profession. No profession is an advantage everywhere, and few are a necessity everywhere. Some just sit closer to one end in more companies than others. At a software company, software engineering is the product itself. As the cost of building falls, [[cheaper-execution-raises-the-cost-of-not-executing|the cost of not building rises]], so they’ll keep building more. At a consumer brand, the same engineering work is a necessity. The brand builds a good-enough store and stops. Design is the advantage for a fashion brand that competes on taste, and a necessity for a B2B distributor that just needs a usable catalog. The clearest advantage cases are the people doing a company’s R&D, whatever it’s called: scientists at a pharma company, chefs at a restaurant group. Their job is, by definition, to find the original insight the company competes on. But the same scientist doing quality control, or the same chef running a corporate cafeteria, is doing necessity work.

<Figure name="profession-map" />

None of this is entirely new. Geoffrey Moore split a company’s activities into _core_ and _context_. Core is what differentiates you, context is everything else, and companies should outsource or automate context.[^6] AI automates more context than any earlier technology. What’s new is how clearly the same profession can be core in one company and context in the next. In “IT Doesn’t Matter”, Nicholas Carr made the electricity argument about IT. Once a technology is available to everyone, it becomes a cost of doing business.[^7] Critics pointed to Walmart, Amazon, and Google, where IT clearly _was_ the advantage. Both sides were right, because it depended on whether IT was core. And Wardley Mapping’s evolution axis has capabilities moving from genesis to custom to product to commodity.[^8] AI speeds up that last step for a whole class of knowledge work.

## 5. Two moves to stay relevant

If AI absorbs execution, and necessity work converges into execution, staying relevant means doing work whose intent is still open. Put the two spectrums together and you get a simple map:

|               | **Execution**                                                                                                                  | **Strategy**                                                                                    |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Advantage** | Execution still gets cheap, so there’s pressure on headcount and price.                                                        | Safest. This is where original insight about how the company competes happens.                  |
| **Necessity** | Most exposed. AI replaces it outright, as with a consumer brand’s storefront, routine contract review, or first-line support. | Exposed. There’s little demand, because the intent converges on the standard answer and leaves little to decide. |

The rows decide whether the _market_ for your work grows or shrinks. The columns decide whether _you_ capture value in it. The natural path is up and to the right. The order matters, so move up first and then right.

<Figure name="strategy-map" />

**Move up.** Work in an area that is a source of competitive advantage for the company you work for, or will become one. Strategy is only in demand there. There are two ways to do it. You can _change the function_, moving into adjacent work that’s hard for AI to replicate. Design is the obvious candidate, because AI still has very limited taste and [[the-new-luxury-is-about-taste-not-wealth|taste increasingly sets brands apart]]. But it only helps if design is an advantage _for that company_. To a company that doesn’t compete on taste, design is just another necessity. Or you can _change the company_ and do the same work for a company that competes on it. The engineer at a consumer brand moves to a software company, and the designer at a distributor moves to a fashion house. Either way, “AI can’t do it well yet” is a statement about today’s models, not a moat. If you move up but stay on the execution side, you’ve found a better market, but you aren’t safe.

**Move right.** Within that area, do more strategic work and less execution. Being in the right context protects the _function_, not everyone in it. Even where a function is an advantage, AI still makes execution cheap. That doesn’t mean fewer people. As the cost of execution falls, the cost of not executing rises, so companies that compete on a function will do far more of it. Software companies will build more software than ever, and because [[ai-moves-validation-downstream|AI moves validation downstream]], they’ll test more ideas by shipping them. But more execution needs more coordination and more judgment about what to build, for whom, in what order, and whether it’s any good. That work still needs humans. The demand for people may well grow. What changes is what those people do. But the supply of people chasing that work will grow faster. AI creates new roles, but everyone pushed out of execution and out of necessities is heading for the same corner of the map, and there won’t be enough roles for all of them.

Moving right only pays after moving up. In a necessity, demand for strategy is thin by definition, because the company doesn’t compete on the function and the intent converges on the standard answer anyway. Moving right without moving up means getting better at something nobody is asking for.

The second move is what people mean when they say “AI won’t take your job; somebody using AI will.”[^9] But the slogan undersells it. It suggests you keep the same job and pick up a new tool. In reality, you may have to change what your job _is_ altogether.

Take software engineering. We’re not far from a world where AI can execute complex software projects end to end, with little to no human supervision. In that world, the traditional software engineer, who spends most of the day turning specs into code, may be gone from most companies, even the ones where software is the advantage. The work shifts upstream, toward deciding what software must be built, for whom, and why, and toward orchestrating far more execution than any team could do by hand. There may be as many people doing that as there were engineers, or more. But that isn’t the same profession adopting AI. It’s a different profession.[^10]

Not everyone will like that, and not everyone who was great at the old job will be great at the new one. Technological change doesn’t have to destroy jobs in aggregate to destroy the specific job you were good at. And nobody can opt out. The models won’t get worse because we’d prefer they did.

And you don’t make this move once. As models get more capable and cheaper, the line between what AI can and can’t do keeps moving up and to the right with you. Work that took judgment last year becomes execution this year, and execution that was too expensive to automate becomes cheap enough. To stay ahead, you have to keep moving.

## 6. Is strategy safe?

Everything so far assumes strategy is where AI stops. We can’t know that for sure.

Consider where original insight comes from. Raw intelligence is close to a commodity. Companies hire it on the open market, and many people could fill any given role. What’s unique is the context. And the company doesn’t own context in isolation. [[context-is-relational|Context comes from the relationship]] between the company and the people in it. The company brings its history, customers, capabilities, and competitive environment. The person brings their past experience, the other companies and industries they’ve seen, and the particular way they relate to this company and its environment. Original insight is what happens when the two meet.

That context is also mostly _illegible_. People rarely write it down. It lives in their heads, in hallway conversations, and in a feel for what customers will tolerate and which initiatives the organization will get behind. David Autor calls this Polanyi’s paradox, summed up as “we know more than we can tell”, and argues it has historically been the main barrier to automating work.[^11] A person absorbs this [[tacit-knowledge-is-hard-to-capture|tacit knowledge]] by being immersed in it. An LLM only has what someone feeds into it, so it reasons from a thin, legible slice of the context and lands closer to the standard answer.

The research so far fits this picture. Csaszar, Ketkar, and Kim found that, on paper, LLMs can generate and evaluate business strategies about as well as entrepreneurs and investors.[^12] But Si, Hashimoto, and Yang had experts spend over 100 hours executing research ideas. Reviewers had rated the LLM ideas _more_ novel than the human ones, but after execution the LLM ideas lost much of their score, and [[ideas-look-better-before-theyre-executed|the ranking flipped]].[^13] Felin and Holweg offer a reason. AI reasons from data, so it’s backward-looking and imitative. Humans reason from theory and can hold beliefs the data doesn’t support yet, like the Wright brothers believing in heavier-than-air flight when the evidence said otherwise.[^14]

<Figure name="ideation-gap" />

None of this is a guarantee. Context can become more legible, since companies record, transcribe, and log more of their work every year. Models may also get better at inferring what isn’t said. There’s no principled reason a model, immersed deeply enough in a company’s context, couldn’t find some of the same insights a person would. This is Tan’s “(for now)” again.

So the essay’s claim isn’t that strategy is safe forever. It’s a claim about [[ai-absorbs-work-in-a-predictable-order|_order_]]. AI absorbs work from the execution end first, and necessities before advantages, because that’s where the standard answer is good enough. The work that depends most on unique context goes last. Whether “last” means never or in fifteen years, you should move toward that end of the map now.

And even if AI gets there, the relational nature of context still matters. A model can be given everything a company has written down. It can’t be given the career you brought with you, or how that career interacts with this particular company. Once raw intelligence is a model anyone can use, the advantage comes from context, and part of that context comes from you.

## 7. Conclusion

Return to the factory. Most factories stopped making their own power, and nobody missed it. The capability didn’t vanish. It moved to the companies for whom power was the product, and the factories focused on what they competed on.

AI is doing the same to knowledge work. It won’t take over whole professions. It takes over execution, starting where the standard answer is good enough. In a necessity, the standard answer is all anyone wants.

So “Will AI replace my profession?” is the wrong question. There are two better ones. Does my work matter to how my company competes, or is it just necessary? And am I executing, or deciding what gets executed? If the answers are “just necessary” and “executing”, you know which way to move. The profession you trained for may not survive the change, but deciding what’s worth doing will be the last work to go.
